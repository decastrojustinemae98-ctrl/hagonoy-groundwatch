Hagonoy, Bulacan · Environmental awareness

The ground under Hagonoy is sinking.

Land subsidence is the slow lowering of the ground surface. In Hagonoy it makes floods deeper, lets high tides reach homes, and cracks the roads people use every day. This site explains why it happens and what the community can do.

About land subsidence

What is land subsidence, and why Hagonoy?

Subsidence is the gradual downward movement of the ground surface. It can come from natural processes, such as soft sediment compacting under its own weight, and from human activity, mostly pumping too much groundwater.

A delta town

Hagonoy sits on the low Manila Bay coast. Its municipal center is only about 6 m above sea level, and the town has 26 barangays and 133,448 residents (2020 census). Soft delta sediment compacts easily.

Sinking and rising together

The ground goes down while the sea comes up. Philippine sea level is rising roughly three times faster than the global average of 3.6 mm a year, so the gap between land and tide closes quickly.

Measured from space

Researchers track the ground with satellite radar (InSAR) and GNSS stations. Studies of Hagonoy and Calumpit found sinking of about 65 to 125 mm each year, depending on the place and the method.

Causes and effects

One cause underneath, three effects on top

Choose a topic to see how groundwater extraction, flooding and infrastructure damage connect.

Main cause

Pumping water out faster than it refills

When water is taken from underground layers faster than rain and rivers can refill them, the pores in the soil collapse and the ground above settles. This cannot be undone once the clay has compacted.

In Hagonoy and Calumpit, satellite data showed faster sinking close to pumping stations than farther away. A regression model using groundwater level, population density and built-up volume explained most of the pattern (adjusted R² of 0.88). Urban growth adds weight and demand on top of the pumping.

70.2 mm/yr

average sinking within 500 m of pumping stations, compared with 65.3 mm/yr farther away.

Natural compaction of delta sediment and tectonic movement also contribute, but human pumping is the part people can change.

Effect

The same rain, a deeper flood

When land sinks it sits closer to river and tide levels. Floods from typhoons and the habagat get deeper and stay longer, and high tides alone can push seawater into streets. A study of Metro Manila by the National Water Resources Board linked heavy extraction to floods that take longer to go down.

In August 2023, floodwater in Hagonoy reached chest level in homes, and families without an upper floor had no safe place to wait it out.

3× a week

how often high tides flood the streets of Pugad, Hagonoy, with water up to 1.5 m on the worst days.

Effect

Roads, houses and bridges move

The ground does not sink evenly. Uneven settling cracks foundations, tilts walls and breaks pavement, and the 2004 NWRB study warned that sudden changes in ground level may damage buildings, bridges and highways.

In Hagonoy and Obando, roads and homes are sinking and residents raise their houses or give up the lower floor. Pugad raises its roads about every three years.

₱200,000

roughly what one Pugad family has spent raising their house every year since 2022.

Seawater intrusion

Heavy pumping near the coast can draw salt water into freshwater layers, which damages wells and farmland.

Sea level rise

Subsidence does not cause sea level rise, but the two add up. The DENR says Philippine sea level rise could accelerate to 13 mm a year.

Community impact

Living with water at the door

The effects are not abstract. They change schedules, budgets and jobs.

Daily life follows the tide

In Pugad, school timetables are set by tide charts to lower the risk of flood-borne disease. Homes stand on stilts and small shops use high tables. About 2,500 people live in the island's only village.

Money goes into raising, not growing

Families keep paying to lift their houses and the barangay keeps paying to raise its roads. Money that could support livelihoods is spent staying above water.

Health and safety

Repeated flooding exposes families to contaminated water. After the August 2023 floods, a resident of a flooded Bulacan barangay said family members had suffered foot wounds from repeated flooding.

Livelihood and place

Boatman Rodolfo Tamayo said leaving is not a real option: "We can't go to [other places], we won't have jobs there. We will go hungry." Village captain Jaime Gregorio said it is already hard to imagine life returning to normal.

Quotes and details are from news reports listed on the References page. Local leadership changes have also made long-term flood measures inconsistent, according to the same reports.

Mitigation and solutions

Subsidence can be slowed. Some of it can be stopped.

Geologist Mahar Lagmay has said subsidence from pumping could be reversed with effective government policy on well digging. Sea level rise needs a wider, global response.

Community actions

What neighbors can do

  • Do not dig unregistered deep wells, and report illegal ones to the barangay or NWRB.
  • Connect to piped or bulk water where it is offered.
  • Report cracks, sinking roads and flood depths using the form on this site.
  • Plan houses and evacuation routes with upper floors and raised storage.
  • Keep canals and waterways clear of garbage.
Government initiatives

What is already in place

  • The National Water Resources Board regulates extraction and sealed 1,008 illegal or abandoned deep wells from 2008 to 2013.
  • Groundwater extraction is restricted in parts of Bulacan. Reports differ on the start year (2004 or 2015).
  • Hagonoy was among seven water districts that signed for the Bulacan Bulk Water Supply Project in 2021, meant to reduce reliance on deep wells.
  • Researchers installed six low-cost GNSS stations in high-subsidence areas of the Bulacan and Pampanga delta.
  • A national government study on the way forward is not expected until 2028.
Sustainable practices

Longer-term fixes

  • Shift from groundwater to surface-water supply.
  • Harvest rainwater for non-drinking use to cut pumping.
  • Protect and replant mangroves and nipa along the coast to soften waves.
  • Include subsidence risk in land-use plans and building permits.
  • Keep local monitoring going so changes are caught early.

Researchers call localized subsidence monitoring an urgent need and recommend better data from water utilities and the Philippine Statistics Authority, plus subsidence risk in land-use planning (Maclang et al., 2026). The rainwater, mangrove and canal items are widely used good practices, not findings of a single Hagonoy study.

Frequently asked questions

Is land subsidence the same as a sinkhole?

No. A sinkhole is a sudden collapse in one spot. Subsidence is slow and wide, often just millimeters or centimeters a year, which is why it is easy to miss until floods get worse.

Can the ground come back up?

Mostly not. Once clay layers compact after water is removed, they do not expand fully again. That is why stopping the pumping early matters. Lagmay has said policy on well digging could reverse the trend, meaning stop further sinking.

Is my own well a problem?

One household well is small, but thousands of wells together can drain the aquifer. Using piped water where available and registering any well with the proper office helps.

Why does it flood even when it did not rain much?

High tides can push seawater into low areas. In Pugad, tide flooding happens at least three times a week.

How do scientists measure it?

Satellites such as Sentinel-1 compare radar images over time (InSAR), and ground stations (GNSS) and surveyed benchmarks check the results.

Where can I get official hazard information?

Project NOAH of the University of the Philippines, the Mines and Geosciences Bureau, the NWRB and your municipal DRRM office.
